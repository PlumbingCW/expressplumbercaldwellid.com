Tankless Water Heater Efficiency for Caldwell, ID Homes

A tankless water heater is usually more efficient than a tank because it does not keep a full tank of water hot all day. Efficiency is rated by a number called UEF, and a higher number means less energy lost for the same hot water.

The savings depend on how much hot water your household uses, what fuel you burn and how cold the incoming water is, which matters in a Caldwell winter.

What UEF tells you

UEF stands for uniform energy factor, a federal rating of how much of the energy a heater uses ends up as hot water. A higher number means less waste, and the label lets you compare one model against another.

Condensing tankless models capture extra heat from exhaust and reach the highest ratings. Intermountain Gas Company's residential tankless rebate, for example, sets a 0.91 UEF minimum, which shows the bar for the most efficient gas units.

Cold winters change the math

NOAA normals for the nearby Nampa station show a January average low near 23 degrees F and about 132 days a year at or below freezing. Water entering the house in winter is colder, so a tankless heater has to add more heat to reach the same temperature, and its maximum flow drops.

In practice a unit sized for summer may struggle with two showers in January. Ask the plumber to size the unit for winter water, not the spring average, and consider a larger model if your household is busy in the morning.

Rebates, scale and Caldwell homes

Rebates for efficient gas heaters come from gas utilities rather than the city. Intermountain Gas Company's tankless rebate requires a minimum 0.91 UEF and an application within 90 days of installation. Ask your own gas provider whether a program applies to you, because we do not name a provider for Caldwell addresses.

About 56 percent of Caldwell's homes were built in 2000 or later, according to Census Bureau estimates, and many have gas lines sized for a tank. Upsizing the line is often the largest extra cost of switching to tankless.

Upkeep protects the efficiency

Scale on the heat exchanger acts like insulation, so efficiency slips as it builds. Yearly flushing through isolation valves removes it, and in hard water a softener or a descaling filter may be worth it.

Caldwell's city reports do not always list hardness, so test your tap or ask the water department at 208-455-3070. If your water is hard, see water softener installation for how a unit fits in.

Small habits that cut hot water use

The cheapest efficiency gain is using less hot water. A low flow showerhead, a lower setpoint of around 120 degrees F and shorter showers all reduce the load on any heater.

Insulating the first few feet of hot water pipe and fixing dripping faucets also help. A hot water leak wastes both the water and the energy used to heat it, and fixing it is a quick win.

Reading the manufacturer's numbers

Manufacturers publish maximum flow rates at a given temperature rise. Read those tables against your winter incoming water temperature, not the number in the brochure headline.

Ask the installer to show the flow at your expected rise, and ask what happens when two fixtures run at once. A model that falls short in January may be fine in July, and you live in both seasons.

Tankless compared with a heat pump

Heat pump water heaters are electric tanks that move heat from the air, and they can use far less electricity than a standard electric tank. They cool the room they sit in and need open space, which suits a garage but may not suit a closet.

A gas tankless unit makes sense when gas is already available and the household wants endless hot water. A heat pump makes sense when the house is all electric and the unit has room. The best option is the one that matches your fuel and your space, as covered on the water heater installation page.
