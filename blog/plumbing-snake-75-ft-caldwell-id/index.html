Using a 75 Foot Plumbing Snake in a Caldwell, ID Home

A 75 foot plumbing snake can reach a main line clog from an outside cleanout, but only if the pipe is sound and you can find the cleanout. It does not clear a coating of grease, and it can damage old cast iron or clay pipe if pushed too hard.

Here is where a long snake works, where it fails and when a plumber with a camera is the safer choice.

What a 75 foot snake is meant for

A snake, or drain auger, is a coiled steel cable with a cutting head that turns inside the pipe. A 75 foot length is meant for a main line, where the run from the house to the street is longer than a sink or a toilet can reach.

Most hand cranked or small electric snakes are sized for sinks and tubs. A heavy machine does the job in a main line, and a rented one can be hard to handle without practice.

Finding the cleanout

A cleanout is a capped pipe, often white plastic or black cast iron, near the house foundation or in a basement floor. Take the cap off slowly because water may be backed up behind it, and keep a bucket and towels ready.

If the house has no cleanout, the cable has to go through a toilet, and a long snake in a toilet is more likely to get stuck or scratch the porcelain. Adding a cleanout is worth discussing with a plumber.

Caldwell lines, old pipe and the public main

The Census Bureau's 2020 to 2024 estimates show about 29 percent of Caldwell's homes were built before 1980. Drain lines in those houses can be cast iron or clay, which crack, scale and collect roots, and a cable pushed hard against a weak joint can make a bad pipe worse.

The City of Caldwell runs the public sewer main, which includes more than 300 miles of line, 4,970 manholes and 16 lift stations. If a snake clears your line but the water still backs up on the street, the blockage may be in the public main, and the sewer emergency line is (208) 949-1278.

Using a snake safely

1

Wear protection

Gloves, eye protection and boots, because backed up water is unsanitary.

2

Feed slowly

Let the cable find the clog and do not force it past a hard stop.

3

Rotate and retrieve

Turn the cable to break up the clog and pull it back slowly to catch debris.

4

Flush and check

Run water through the line and watch for slow drainage after the clog clears.

Choosing the right cable size

Cable thickness has to match the pipe. A thin cable for a sink is too light for a main line, and a heavy main-line cable is too stiff to turn a trap.

For a home main line, a three-eighths or half-inch cable is common, with a cutter head chosen for the clog. Ask the rental store which model suits a four-inch line, and ask for a demonstration.

Cleaning up afterward

A snake that has been in a sewer line is dirty and can carry bacteria. Wear gloves, hose off the cable outside and let it dry before you coil it.

Wash your hands and clothing, and clean the area around the cleanout with a disinfectant. Keep children and pets away until everything is dry.

Risks of doing it yourself

A cable can kink, jam or break inside a pipe, and a stuck cable is a bigger problem than the original clog. Old pipe can crack under force, and a crack under a slab is expensive to repair.

A snake also does not show you what is wrong. A repeat clog after snaking means the pipe has roots, a low spot or a coating that needs a different fix, and a camera is the way to see it. See sewer camera inspection.
