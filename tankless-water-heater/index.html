How a tankless heater works and what it needs

A tankless heater fires a burner or energizes elements only when a hot tap is open. Cold water passes through a heat exchanger and leaves hot, in a steady stream, until the flow rate exceeds what the unit can heat.

That is why the sizing and the supply lines matter. A gas model needs a gas line sized for its high input, a vent that can carry the exhaust, and a way to drain the condensate. An electric model needs heavy electrical service that many older panels cannot supply.

Tank versus tankless in a Caldwell home

Standard tank

  • Lower purchase price and a simple swap
  • Keeps a reserve of hot water ready
  • Standby loss while it holds the heat
  • Roughly 8 to 12 years of service

Tankless

  • Higher price and more prep work
  • Heats on demand without a reserve
  • Saves floor space inside a garage or closet
  • Often listed for 15 to 20 years with upkeep

Flushing and descaling: the service most units miss

Scale builds on the heat exchanger and narrows the water path, so the unit works harder and throws error codes. A descale flush runs a mild acid solution through the exchanger with a small pump, then rinses it clear.

Many manufacturers call for a flush about once a year, and sooner in hard water. Caldwell's annual water quality reports for 2024 and 2025 do not list a hardness figure, so a test strip on your own tap tells you how fast scale is likely to grow.

Signs your tankless heater needs service

Error codes on the display

Scale, a clogged inlet screen, a venting fault or low gas pressure can each trip a code. The code and the model number speed the diagnosis.

Water runs cold then hot

A clogged inlet filter, scale in the exchanger or a flow sensor fault can cause cold sandwich swings.

Weak hot flow

A fouled exchanger or an undersized supply line reduces output. Flushing often restores it.

Banging or a fan that screams

A worn fan motor, a blocked vent or an exchanger choked by scale makes the unit loud.

Unit shuts down mid shower

Overheat sensors trip when flow is too low or scale is heavy. A flush usually clears it.

Pilot or ignition failures

On gas models a dirty burner, a failing igniter or a low gas supply can stop ignition.

Gas line and venting: the part that decides the install

A gas tankless heater draws far more fuel at once than a tank heater, and the old gas line is often too small. The plumber measures the run, counts the other appliances on the line and sizes the pipe accordingly.

Venting is the other gate. Some units use PVC or stainless through a wall, and some need a roof termination. The vent route, the clearances and the condensate drain are set before the unit is ordered, not after.

What drives a tankless quote

Tankless jobs vary more than tank swaps because the house decides so much of the work.

Unit and flow rateA higher flow model for a large household costs more than an entry unit.
Gas line upgradeA larger pipe or a new run from the meter adds parts and labor.
VentingA short side vent is simpler than a long run to the roof.
Electrical, for electric unitsA new breaker and heavy wire may be needed.
Condensate and water treatmentA neutralizer and a drain, plus isolation valves for future flushes, are good additions.

When replacement beats repair

A tankless unit under ten years old with a code history of scale is usually a flush and a sensor swap. A unit with a cracked exchanger or years of unmaintained scale may not be worth the repair bill.

If a tank is failing instead, emergency water heater replacement covers the urgent case, and water heater repair handles gas and electric tanks.
